# My Take: The Real AI Revolution in Medicine Isn't Where You Think It Is

Here's my take. We hear a lot about artificial intelligence discovering new drugs — designing molecules, predicting protein structures, compressing years of laboratory work into months. It makes for compelling headlines. But there's a less glamorous problem lurking behind those breakthroughs, and it may be the single biggest obstacle between scientific innovation and the patients who need it most.

The bottleneck isn't discovery. It's development.

Let me explain. Over the past decade, pharmaceutical R&D spending has climbed relentlessly — and yet productivity has remained stubbornly flat. Clinical trial timelines have grown by more than a third. More than half of trial sites report that complexity has increased significantly since 2020. Think about what that means: we are getting faster at finding promising therapies and slower at proving they work. It is as if we built a supersonic jet but left it idling on a runway with no air traffic control.

This is the paradox at the heart of modern pharma. AI is turbocharging the front end of the pipeline while the back end — the clinical trials, the regulatory coordination, the painstaking work of enrolling patients and monitoring outcomes — remains mired in manual processes, fragmented data systems, and organizational silos. Discovery gains are, quite literally, evaporating before they reach a single patient.

Now, the industry is not blind to this. Large pharma companies are running AI pilots. Biotechs are experimenting with niche tools. Contract research organizations are building digital platforms. But here's the catch: only 28 percent of those AI pilots ever make it into production. The rest are what I'd call "innovation theater" — impressive demonstrations that never scale. The tools are disconnected. The improvements are incremental. And clinical development is still held together by the same manual coordination across sponsors, investigators, CROs, and regulators that it was a decade ago.

What's needed is not more pilots. It's orchestration.

The most promising opportunity, according to a recent analysis by Bain & Company, is reimagining the very architecture of clinical trials — starting with protocol design. Today, the teams responsible for the scientific, operational, and competitive dimensions of a trial work in sequence and in silos. By the time one group finishes, the landscape has shifted. AI can collapse those silos. Using digital twins and advanced simulations, sponsors could assess feasibility, cost, timelines, and competitive benchmarks simultaneously, in real time, making smarter trade-offs before a single patient is enrolled.

Only 13 percent of pharma companies are actively piloting this. But among those who are, 75 percent report satisfaction with efficiency gains, and they expect a fourfold return on investment. That's a striking signal — small sample, but a powerful one.

And the financial stakes are enormous. Accelerating clinical development by even 20 to 30 percent can translate into hundreds of millions of dollars in earlier revenue per major drug asset. At the portfolio level, that scales to billions. In an industry where the average cost of bringing a single drug to market now exceeds two billion dollars, these aren't marginal gains. They're transformational.

But here's where I think the conversation gets most interesting — and where the conventional wisdom about AI gets it wrong. The future of clinical trials is not the "autonomous trial." It's not about removing humans from the process. It's about redesigning the role humans play.

Bain's research points to something that should be obvious but is often overlooked: a better experience for investigators leads to faster recruitment and faster trial completion. Past technology transformations in this industry — remote monitoring, risk-based oversight — failed when they leapfrogged trust. When you impose technology on people who don't understand it, don't trust it, and weren't consulted about it, performance doesn't improve. It deteriorates.

The organizations pulling ahead are doing something different. They're co-creating tools with the people who use them — site monitors, coordinators, investigators, patients. They're running measurable pilots that prove value before demanding adoption. They're building what Bain calls "a stronger judgment layer" — not thinner teams, but teams that make faster, safer decisions because AI handles the routine coordination while humans exercise oversight and accountability.

This is a model I find deeply compelling, and not just for pharma. Across every industry grappling with AI adoption, the winners will not be the ones who automate the most aggressively. They will be the ones who understand that the real bottleneck is almost never the technology. It's the organizational design, the data infrastructure, the trust deficit between humans and machines.

Consider the data problem alone. Only about 20 percent of clinical trial data is actually analyzed. The rest sits in silos — inaccessible, unstandardized, unlabeled. Nearly half of pharmaceutical companies cite data readiness as their top obstacle in scaling AI. You can build the most sophisticated agentic system in the world, but if it's reasoning over fragmented, incomplete data, you'll get fragmented, incomplete results.

So what should leaders do? The Bain blueprint is pragmatic: pick two or three high-value workflows with clear pain points. Don't chase perfect data — chase usable data. Build orchestrated systems with humans in the loop at every critical juncture. And — perhaps most importantly — make a strategic choice. Decide whether you're going to win through site-facing enablement, internal operations transformation, protocol reinvention, or full end-to-end lighthouse trials. Don't try to do everything. Do one thing at enterprise scale.

There is a broader lesson here that extends well beyond the pharmaceutical industry. We are living through the early stages of the most significant technological transformation since the internet. And yet the pattern repeating itself across sectors is familiar: the technology races ahead, while the institutions, processes, and human systems that must absorb it lag behind. The organizations that close that gap — not by moving faster, but by moving smarter — will define the next era.

In pharma, the cost of getting this wrong isn't just financial. It's measured in patients waiting for treatments that already exist in a laboratory but haven't yet survived the gauntlet of clinical development. That's not an abstraction. That's a moral urgency.

And that's my take.
